# Phase B: Synthetic Media Policy

**Organization:** Patient Outreach Team, Lakeside Regional Health System
**Applies to:** All staff, contractors, and vendors who create or send patient-facing
communications on behalf of the outreach team
**Effective date:** October 1, 2026
**Owner:** Director of Patient Communications, with the Privacy Officer and Chief Medical
Officer as required approvers

## Purpose and Scope

This policy governs whether and how the Patient Outreach Team may use synthetic media,
meaning any audio, video, or image generated or substantially altered by AI tools,
including synthetic voices, cloned voices, AI avatars, and AI-generated visuals.

We are a small team that sends appointment reminders, preparation instructions, general
health education, and administrative notices to a patient population that includes
elderly patients, patients who are ill or frightened, and patients who do not read or
speak English as a first language. Our audience is unusually likely to trust a message
that appears to come from their care team, and unusually unlikely to question it. That
fact shapes every rule below.

The short version of this policy: we may use clearly labeled synthetic voice for a narrow
set of routine administrative messages. We do not use synthetic media for anything
clinical, and we never clone the voice or face of a real person. When in doubt, we do not
use it.

## 1. Permitted Uses

Synthetic media is permitted only for the following, and only when every condition in the
later sections (disclosure, provenance, review) is met:

- **Appointment reminders** delivered by a clearly labeled synthetic voice, limited to
  the date, time, location, and a request to call the office with questions. No medical
  content.
- **Logistical and administrative notices**: parking and facility information, office
  closures, insurance-paperwork reminders, general "your results are ready, please call
  the office" messages that contain no result and no clinical detail.
- **General, non-personalized health education** using a synthetic voice or a fully
  synthetic (not real-person) avatar, for content that is already approved public
  material, such as "what to bring to your first visit." This content must be reviewed
  and approved as clinical education before any synthetic version is made.

A use falls inside the boundary only if a reasonable reader would agree it contains no
diagnosis, no test result, no medication instruction, no personalized medical advice, and
no real person's likeness. If any of those are present, the use is not permitted, even
with disclosure.

## 2. Prohibited Uses

The following are refused outright, regardless of intent, disclosure, or convenience:

- **Cloning the voice or face of any real person**, including our own physicians, nurses,
  and staff, even with their permission. We do not create a synthetic version of a real
  clinician for any purpose. This is a firm line and section 8 explains why we do not make
  exceptions to it.
- **Any clinical communication**: diagnoses, test or lab results, medication names, doses,
  or instructions, treatment recommendations, pre- or post-procedure medical instructions,
  or anything a patient could reasonably act on medically.
- **Personalized medical content of any kind** delivered by a synthetic voice or avatar,
  even if the content is administrative in tone, if it reveals or implies a specific
  patient's condition.
- **Emotionally persuasive or reassuring synthetic delivery** designed to calm, urge, or
  influence a patient's medical decision. We do not use a warm synthetic voice to make a
  message feel more trustworthy than its source warrants.
- **Synthetic media aimed at exploiting or pressuring vulnerable patients**, including
  fundraising or upselling of services delivered through a synthetic clinician-like voice.
- **Any synthetic artifact without the disclosure and provenance required below.**

## 3. Consent Workflow

Because we do not clone real people, most of this policy does not involve personal
consent. The consent rules exist to enforce that boundary, not to create a path around
it.

- **No real-person voice or likeness may be captured, cloned, or synthesized under this
  policy.** There is no consent form that unlocks this. A clinician cannot volunteer their
  voice for cloning, because the risk described in section 8 is not theirs alone to waive.
- **Voice talent for our synthetic voices** must come from a licensed stock or vendor
  voice that is not a real, identifiable individual associated with our organization, or
  from a voice actor who has signed a written release specifically covering synthetic
  generation, the scope of use, the duration, and a revocation process. That release is
  held by the Privacy Officer.
- **Patients are never asked to consent to receive synthetic media as a condition of
  care.** Any patient may request a human-delivered version of any message, and we honor
  that request without friction.

## 4. Disclosure Standards

- Every synthetic voice message begins with a plain spoken statement: "This is an
  automated message from Lakeside Regional Health System using a computer-generated voice." No exceptions, and
  it is not buried at the end.
- Every synthetic video or avatar carries an on-screen label reading "AI-generated" that
  is visible for the full duration, not just a title card, because as I found in Task 6, a
  clip taken from the middle loses anything shown only at the start.
- Written or app-delivered synthetic content carries a visible "AI-generated" tag next to
  the play control.
- We assume disclosure will sometimes be stripped downstream. That is a reason to limit
  what we put into synthetic form in the first place, not a reason to rely on the label.
  Content that would be dangerous if the label were removed does not get made, even with
  the label.

## 5. Provenance Requirements

- All synthetic outputs must carry content credentials (C2PA or equivalent) recording the
  tool, date, and that the content is AI-generated, where the tool supports it.
- Where the tool applies a visible watermark, it stays on. It is never cropped or covered.
  My Task 6 work showed a baked-in watermark is more durable than metadata, so we prefer
  tools that watermark the pixels.
- We keep an internal record for every synthetic artifact we release: the script, the
  tool and version, the approver, the release date, and the intended audience. If a piece
  of content is ever questioned, we can show exactly what we made and what we did not.
- We do not treat provenance as proof against misuse. It lets us prove what we made. It
  does nothing against someone forging content in our name, which is addressed in section
  7.

## 6. Review and Approval

- No synthetic artifact is produced or released without sign-off from the Director of
  Patient Communications and, for any content touching health information, the Chief
  Medical Officer or their delegate.
- The reviewer applies one test first: does this contain anything clinical or any real
  person's likeness? If yes, it is refused at review and does not proceed.
- The reviewer then confirms disclosure and provenance requirements are met and that the
  script matches approved source material word for word. Synthetic delivery may not
  introduce any wording that was not approved.
- Batch or automated sends (for example, reminders generated from a scheduling system)
  require approval of the template and the data source, not each message, but the template
  approval must be re-confirmed whenever the script or the data source changes. This is the
  scale risk from my Phase A analysis: one wrong template becomes thousands of wrong
  messages, so the template is where review has to be strict.

## 7. Incident Response

When something produced under this policy is misused, misattributed, reaches an
unintended audience, or is forged in our name:

- Any staff member who becomes aware of it reports it to the Director of Patient
  Communications and the Privacy Officer the same day.
- We pause the relevant synthetic-media use immediately while we assess.
- If patients may have received wrong or misattributed information, we contact affected
  patients with a human-delivered correction, not another automated message.
- If a third party has forged or altered our content, we use our internal provenance
  records to document what we actually released, notify the platform hosting the content,
  and, where patient safety or the law requires, notify the appropriate authorities.
- We record what happened and revise this policy if the incident revealed a gap.

## 8. Refusal

This organization declines to use synthetic media whenever:

- The content is clinical, personalized-medical, or would be dangerous if its label were
  removed.
- The use would require cloning a real person's voice or face. We refuse this even when
  the person consents, because a cloned clinician voice, once it exists, can be misused by
  others, can outlive the clinician's relationship with the hospital, and puts the
  clinician's professional identity into messages they did not personally review. The risk
  is not only to the patient who might be deceived but to the clinician whose identity is
  now speaking without them. No single person can meaningfully consent away that risk on
  behalf of every future patient, so we do not offer the option.
- The team is uncertain whether a use is safe. Uncertainty resolves toward not using the
  technology. A message delivered a little less efficiently by a human is always an
  acceptable outcome here.

A note on why the permitted set is so narrow: we could technically do far more. The tools
are cheap, fast, and convincing. This policy treats that ease as a reason for caution
rather than a reason to expand, because our audience trusts us and is not well positioned
to protect itself. We would rather be a team that under-uses this capability than one that
finds out, after the fact, that a labeled convenience became an unlabeled harm somewhere
downstream.

## Limitations

This policy has real limits, and pretending otherwise would make it worse, not better.

**It relies on people acting in good faith at the point of creation.** Every rule here
assumes staff run content through review. A well-intentioned employee under time pressure
could send a "quick reminder" without approval, or could word an administrative message so
that it drifts into clinical territory ("your results are ready and you'll want to see your
doctor soon") without realizing they crossed a line. The boundary between administrative
and clinical is clear in the obvious cases and genuinely fuzzy at the edges, and the policy
cannot fully define that edge in advance.

**Disclosure and provenance protect the original, not the copy.** As my Phase A analysis
and my Task 6 experience both showed, a label or watermark can survive some handling and
still be stripped by a determined edit or a platform that discards metadata. Once our
content leaves our channels, we do not control what happens to it. The policy manages this
by limiting what we put into synthetic form, but it cannot eliminate the risk.

**It does nothing against a bad actor forging content in our name.** Nothing in this
policy stops a scammer from cloning a generic "hospital" voice and calling our patients.
Our provenance records let us prove what we did release, which helps after the fact, but
the policy is a set of rules for us, not a shield around our patients. The most it can do
is avoid adding to the problem and avoid training our patients to expect synthetic calls
from us, which is one reason the permitted set is so small.

**The refusal on voice cloning may cost us something real.** Patients probably would
respond better to a familiar doctor's voice. By refusing that outright, we accept lower
engagement in exchange for a firm line. That is a deliberate trade, and a future decision
maker could reasonably question it. I am comfortable with the trade, but I want to name it
as a choice rather than pretend there is no cost.

**Good-faith reliance is not a flaw I can engineer away.** Any policy governing human
behavior relies on people mostly following it. I do not think that reliance is fatal here,
because the permitted uses are so limited that the damage from a slip is bounded. But it is
honest to say that this policy produces vigilance, not a guarantee. That is the most any
policy in this space can currently offer, and a policy that claimed more would be the more
dangerous document.
